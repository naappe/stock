# Tourism Earns the Dollars. Why Can’t Maldivians Get Them?

## A practical proposal for making Maldives’ tourism dollars work for Maldivians

Maldives has an unusual economic problem. Millions of tourists visit our country and spend billions of US dollars, yet an ordinary Maldivian can still struggle to obtain a few hundred dollars from the official banking system.

This matters because Maldives cannot avoid foreign currency. We import much of our food, fuel, medicine, machinery, construction material, vehicles and other essential goods. Businesses need dollars to pay overseas suppliers. Families need dollars for education, medical treatment and travel. Government needs dollars for foreign debt and other external payments.

> If tourism earns billions of dollars, why are official dollars still difficult for Maldivians to obtain?

The answer is not simply that Maldives fails to earn dollars. The problem is what happens between the tourist spending the dollar and the public trying to buy that dollar.

---

## The numbers explain the problem

Maldives recorded approximately **US$5.57 billion in tourism/travel receipts in 2025**.

Goods imports were around **US$3.62 billion**, while workers’ remittance outflows were approximately **US$661 million**.

The MMA Governor has estimated that roughly **US$1.5–1.6 billion of tourism-sector foreign-currency earnings remains outside the domestic banking system**.

Businesses converted approximately **US$670.8 million through commercial banks in 2025** under the Foreign Currency Act. MMA separately recorded around **US$492 million in mandatory-conversion foreign-currency inflows** received during the year.

These numbers describe different stages of the FX system. They should not simply be added or subtracted from one another. But together they show the central problem:

> Maldives earns a lot of dollars, but only part of those dollars becomes usable official-market liquidity.

Recent MMA figures add another important distinction. Around **65% of tourism foreign currency entered domestic banks in 2025**, rising to approximately **73% by June 2026**. However, the amount tourism businesses actually sold or exchanged to banks was much smaller — around **21% in 2025** and approximately **20% during the first half of 2026**.

A dollar entering a bank account is therefore not automatically a dollar available for the public to buy.

---

## Follow one tourist’s US$1,000

Imagine a tourist spends **US$1,000** at a resort.

Most people naturally think: “Maldives received US$1,000.”

But the resort may need to pay foreign booking commissions, overseas suppliers, foreign debt, management costs, imported equipment, taxes and domestic expenses.

Some of the US$1,000 may stay in a USD account. Some may leave Maldives again. Some may be converted into MVR. Some may reach MMA. Only part may eventually become available to another Maldivian trying to buy dollars.

That is why **tourism revenue is not the same as public dollar availability**.

---

## Dollars in a bank are not automatically for sale

Suppose a resort has **US$1 million in a USD account at a Maldivian bank**.

That does not mean the bank has US$1 million of free surplus dollars that it can immediately sell to the public. The bank still owes that foreign currency to its customer and must maintain sufficient FX liquidity for withdrawals, transfers and other obligations.

The resort may later use those dollars to pay overseas suppliers, foreign debt, insurance, commissions or other external costs.

So we must distinguish between:

**USD deposited in Maldives**

and

**USD actually sold into the official FX market.**

---

## The offshore settlement gap

Imagine a Maldivian resort receives a **US$5,000** tourist payment through an international travel agent or payment processor.

Under an offshore-first model, the payment can first settle outside Maldives. Foreign commissions, debt and supplier payments may be paid before the remaining amount later enters Maldives.

A stronger model would reverse the order:

**Tourist pays US$5,000**

→ **US$5,000 first becomes visible in a Maldives-regulated settlement system**

→ verified foreign commissions, debt, suppliers and other legitimate costs can leave transparently.

Now policy makers can see, for example:

**US$5,000 IN**

and

**US$2,300 OUT.**

The principle should be:

> Tourism-generated foreign currency should first become visible inside the Maldivian financial system before legitimate external payments leave the country.

This should apply across licensed banks — not only BML.

---

## Aim for 100% visibility, not 100% forced conversion

The solution should not be confiscation.

A resort genuinely needs foreign currency for overseas suppliers, booking commissions, foreign debt, insurance and international management expenses.

There is little benefit in forcing a business to convert all of those dollars into MVR only to ask the bank to sell the same business dollars again for a genuine foreign payment.

The better objective is:

**100% visibility**

but not necessarily

**100% forced conversion.**

All tourism FX should first be captured and recorded through the Maldives-regulated system. Genuine external obligations can then leave transparently.

---

## Make MVR the real domestic currency

Maldives should gradually establish a simple economic principle:

> **Inside Maldives = MVR**
>
> **Across the border = foreign currency**

Local suppliers, rent, transport, ordinary domestic services and most local salaries should normally settle in MVR, subject to carefully designed exceptions.

Foreign suppliers, imports, overseas education, medical treatment and external debt should continue to use foreign currency.

This creates natural demand for rufiyaa instead of allowing USD to circulate unnecessarily as a second domestic currency.

Tourists should still be able to use Visa, Mastercard and other international cards. A restaurant can price an item in MVR, the tourist can tap a foreign card, and the merchant can receive MVR through the domestic acquiring and settlement system.

---

## Why the black market survives

BML’s standard published cash buying rate has been around **MVR15.00 per US$1**, while the official selling rate is around **MVR15.42**. Mandatory tourism conversion can receive the 15.42 rate.

Now imagine an unofficial buyer offers **MVR22 per US$1**.

For US$10,000:

At MVR15.42 = **MVR154,200**

At MVR22 = **MVR220,000**

Difference = **MVR65,800**

It is unrealistic to expect people to ignore such a difference.

A black market becomes powerful when two conditions exist together:

1. the unofficial price is much higher; and
2. official dollars are difficult to obtain.

A board showing “USD selling rate: MVR15.42” means little if the next answer is “no dollars available.”

Availability matters.

---

## Government should compete for dollars

Government and licensed banks should make the official market the easiest and safest place to sell foreign currency.

A narrow official spread, instant bank credit, digital records and access through multiple banks and licensed exchangers can make official conversion more attractive.

But government must not create an artificial loss. It cannot sustainably buy dollars at MVR20 and sell the same dollars at MVR15.42. That would create arbitrage and drain public money.

The official system must ultimately reflect economic reality.

---

## Create a national FX liquidity mechanism

Foreign currency should not be treated as isolated pools belonging to separate banks.

Maldives needs a national liquidity mechanism supervised by MMA. The country already has part of this structure through mandatory conversion and FX redistribution.

The flow can be strengthened into:

**Tourism/business FX**

→ commercial banks

→ **MMA / national FX liquidity pool**

→ participating banks

→ citizens and legitimate businesses.

---

## Give the public real official dollar access

A national FX platform could be accessible through participating bank apps.

Users could select:

- Travel
- Medical
- Education
- Business import
- Other approved international payment

### Traveller example

Ahmed is travelling to Sri Lanka and needs **US$500**.

Passport and ticket are verified. At MVR15.42, the cost is **MVR7,710**. The official FX can be credited to a travel card or account, with an authorised amount of cash available when genuinely required.

### Medical example

A family needs **US$5,000** for overseas treatment. The hospital invoice is verified and the official system transfers the money directly to the hospital.

### Business example

A shop needs **US$20,000** to import food. The importer submits the supplier invoice and required business documents. Official FX is transferred directly to the overseas supplier.

These mechanisms meet genuine needs while reducing opportunities for resale and abuse.

---

## Where are the dollars going?

The dollars are not simply disappearing.

Maldives imported approximately **US$3.62 billion of goods in 2025**. Workers’ remittance outflows were around **US$661 million**.

MMA also reported approximately **US$1.1 billion in FX-market intervention during 2025**. Around **US$650.9 million went to SOEs**, including needs connected with fuel, staple food, medicine, medical equipment and external obligations. Around **US$447.6 million was provided through commercial banks for individuals and businesses**.

Government external debt and other overseas payments also consume substantial foreign currency.

So the national question must be both:

> How do we bring more dollars into the official system?

and

> How much are we sending out, and why?

---

## Publish a National Dollar Dashboard

MMA should publish a simple monthly FX statement showing:

- tourism FX entering banks;
- FX actually converted;
- FX transferred to MMA;
- government FX revenue;
- SOE allocation;
- essential imports;
- public and business allocation;
- foreign debt payments;
- remittances and other outflows;
- reserve increases or decreases.

The public should be able to ask one simple question and get a numerical answer:

> **Where did the dollars go?**

Transparency would reduce rumours and make economic policy easier to judge.

---

## Maldives has an import problem too

Even a perfect FX system cannot permanently solve the problem if Maldives continuously needs more foreign currency than it earns.

Every dollar we avoid spending unnecessarily overseas is economically similar to earning another dollar.

Renewable energy can reduce imported fuel. Viable local agriculture can reduce food-import demand. Higher-value fisheries can increase foreign-currency earnings. More local tourism procurement can keep tourism dollars circulating inside Maldives for longer.

The dollar problem is therefore also an energy, agriculture, fisheries, industrial-policy, debt and government-spending problem.

The long-term objective should be:

> **Earn more dollars and need fewer dollars.**

---

## What does the ordinary Maldivian gain?

A US$100 imported product costs **MVR1,542 at 15.42** but **MVR2,200 at 22** — a difference of **MVR658**.

That additional FX cost eventually reaches consumers.

Reliable official FX can therefore reduce one source of import-price pressure. It can also improve access to medicine, education and travel; create fairer competition between businesses; reduce the power of black-market networks; strengthen reserves; and improve public trust through transparent data.

---

## What government should not do

Government should not:

- confiscate all resort dollars;
- force 100% conversion regardless of genuine foreign costs;
- give BML a monopoly;
- ban international tourist cards;
- promise unlimited US$15.42 dollars without adequate supply;
- buy dollars above the rate at which it sells them;
- depend only on enforcement;
- hide government and SOE FX usage;
- rely on borrowing simply to make reserves look stronger.

Government should instead act as regulator, transparent market-maker, allocator and reserve manager.

---

## The final test: can MVR15.42 be sustained?

After improving capture and transparency, Maldives can finally test the exchange rate using real numbers.

Suppose monthly official FX inflow is **US$450 million** while legitimate demand is **US$380 million**. There is a **US$70 million surplus**, making reserve accumulation easier.

But suppose inflow is **US$350 million** while legitimate demand is **US$450 million**. There is still a **US$100 million shortage every month**.

Police enforcement cannot erase that arithmetic.

Government would then have to reduce imports and foreign spending, strengthen exports and reserves, manage external debt and, if necessary, examine whether the exchange-rate arrangement itself remains sustainable.

---

## Conclusion

The Maldives dollar problem should not simply be described as “we do not have dollars.”

Maldives earns billions of dollars.

The deeper questions are:

- Where does each tourism dollar first settle?
- How much enters Maldivian banks?
- How much is actually converted?
- How much leaves again?
- How much do government and SOEs consume?
- How much becomes available to ordinary people?

The solution cannot therefore be one government exchange counter.

It requires a complete system:

> **Tourism Dollar → Maldives Financial System → MVR Domestic Economy → National FX Liquidity → Public Dollar Access → Stronger Reserves**

The objective is not to trap every dollar inside Maldives.

The objective is to make tourism dollars visible when they enter, allow legitimate foreign costs transparently, strengthen MVR domestically, make official FX available for genuine needs, reduce unnecessary foreign-currency demand, and show the public exactly where the country’s dollars go.

Tourism already gives Maldives an extraordinary foreign-currency engine. The next challenge is to make that engine provide greater economic security for Maldivian people.
