When does extra duty become overtime?

A practical Maldives worker-rights guide on 8-hour contractual duty, compulsory overtime, planned maintenance, emergency work, rest, attendance records and how to raise a concern without turning it into a confrontation.

Updated 15 September 2026 · Based on the Maldives Employment Act and General Regulation on Employment

The basic distinction: normal duty and overtime are not the same thing

If an employee's employment agreement or job description states a normal duty period, that matters. Maldives employment rules distinguish regular working hours from overtime. The General Regulation on Employment requires employers to record regular hours and overtime separately, including the daily start and end times.

Practical point: If an employer's own attendance system labels additional hours as OT rather than regular duty, that is an important record showing that the additional period is being treated separately from normal working hours.

Section 32 of the Employment Act sets a general maximum of 48 normal working hours per week, excluding overtime carried out in accordance with the Act. Section 37 governs overtime and its payment.

A common extended-shift example

08:00–15:00Normal/early duty period
15:00–00:00Additional evening work
08:00–15:00Duty again the following morning

This creates a 16-hour continuous work period from 08:00 until midnight, followed by only eight hours before the next duty starts. Payment is only one part of the issue. The other questions are whether the extra work was lawfully arranged, whether it falls under an exception, whether breaks/rest are properly managed, and whether fatigue creates a safety concern.

Paying OT does not automatically answer every legal question. Compensation deals with payment. It does not by itself determine whether the additional work was regular duty, agreed overtime, or a genuine emergency exception.

What Section 37 says about overtime

The Employment Act states that employees are not to be required to work overtime unless overtime has been agreed in the employment agreement. The 9th Amendment to the Employment Act, ratified on 14 March 2026, clarified the wording around overtime arrangements.

Overtime must also be paid at the statutory rate: generally 1.25 times the hourly wage on a normal working day and 1.5 times the hourly wage on a Friday or public holiday.

If the contract defines the normal working period, extra hours should not simply be re-labelled as ordinary duty when the employer's own records treat them as overtime.

Emergency work is a real exception — but the facts matter

Section 34 exempts certain categories and emergency situations from some of the normal working-hours and overtime provisions. It also allows regulations to address exceptional circumstances such as accidents, incidents outside the employer's control, and equipment-related work that could significantly harm business operations if it is not carried out beyond normal hours.

Examples that may support an emergency argument

  • An unexpected generator or critical equipment failure.
  • An accident or serious operational incident.
  • An unforeseen event that threatens continuity of an essential operation.
  • Immediate equipment work where delay could cause significant operational harm.

What is not automatically an emergency

  • Routine staffing shortages.
  • A shift that management knew needed coverage in advance.
  • Ordinary planned maintenance simply because it happens outside normal duty hours.
  • A recurring practice of assigning double shifts and describing all of them as emergency work.
Planned maintenance can still become urgent in some circumstances. The correct question is not merely "Was it maintenance?" but "What made this work immediately necessary, and which legal exception is being relied upon?"

Records matter

The General Regulation on Employment requires employers to keep reliable records of regular hours, overtime, sign-in/sign-out times and overtime payments. For an employee concerned about compulsory extra work, contemporaneous records are important.

  • Keep the duty roster.
  • Keep screenshots showing hours classified as OT.
  • Keep attendance records and overtime approvals.
  • Keep messages or written instructions assigning additional duty.
  • Note the date, time and people present when a verbal instruction is given.
  • Record whether the reason given was planned maintenance, staff shortage, breakdown or emergency.

If an employee attends additional work because management instructed them to do so, a neutral written record can prevent the attendance record from later being interpreted as proof that the overtime was volunteered.

Example record: “For record purposes, I attended the additional overtime following management instruction. This was assigned additional duty and was not overtime volunteered by me.”

How to raise the concern without refusing normal duty

A worker who wants to protect their position should distinguish clearly between normal contractual duty and disputed additional overtime.

“I am not refusing my normal contractual duty. I am asking whether this additional duty is agreed overtime under Section 37 or whether management is relying on an emergency or exceptional-work provision under Section 34.”

This approach keeps the discussion focused on the legal and contractual basis of the extra hours rather than allowing the issue to be described as a general refusal to work.

Questions management should be able to answer

  • What are the employee's normal contractual working hours?
  • Is the additional period recorded as overtime?
  • What contract clause or agreement authorises compulsory overtime?
  • If Section 34 is being relied upon, what is the specific emergency or exceptional circumstance?
  • If the work is maintenance, was it planned in advance or did an unexpected fault make immediate work necessary?
  • What rest and safety arrangements apply after an extended shift?

Meal breaks, rest and safety

Section 35 generally provides that an employee should not work more than five consecutive hours without at least a 30-minute meal break, subject to special treatment for employees exempted under Section 34.

The Act also contains workplace health and safety obligations. In safety-critical work, fatigue can be operationally significant. An extended work period followed by a short turnaround before the next duty should therefore be assessed not only as a payroll issue but also as a safety and risk-management issue.

What about leave for missing overtime?

If an employee attends their normal scheduled duty but cannot attend an additional overtime period, the question of whether annual leave, family responsibility leave or another leave category is required should be checked against the employer's policy and the employment agreement. It should not automatically be assumed that missing overtime is the same as being absent from normal contractual duty.

If management requires a leave category to be used solely because an employee did not attend additional overtime, asking for that requirement in writing creates a clear record of the employer's position.

Key takeaway

The strongest argument is not “I do not want OT.”

It is: “My contract defines my normal working hours. The company's own records classify the additional hours as overtime. Please identify the contractual or statutory basis on which this overtime is compulsory, and if an emergency exception is being relied upon, identify the emergency or exceptional circumstance.”

An internal company policy cannot remove statutory rights granted by the Employment Act. At the same time, employment agreements and genuine emergency/exceptional circumstances can materially affect the legal analysis, so the exact contract wording and facts of each incident matter.
